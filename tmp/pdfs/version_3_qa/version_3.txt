Matched WEIRD and Serbian Debates Show No Detectable WEIRD
Advantage in LLM Perspective Coverage
Anonymous TACL submission
Abstract
Large language models can describe politi-
cal controversies accurately while omitting
relevant perspectives. We test whether they
recover fewer documented perspectives on
Serbian controversies than on structurally
similar topics from Western, Educated, In-
dustrialised, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD)
settings. Our benchmark contains 16 topics
in eight matched pairs, with five externally
documented reference perspectives per topic.
Thirteen LLMs each generate five perspec-
tives per topic in English. Five LLM judges
match each output to a reference perspec-
tive or return no match; no judge evaluates a
generator from its own model family. Recall
measures the proportion of reference perspec-
tives recovered. Mean recall was 0.581 for
WEIRD topics and 0.594 for Serbian topics.
The mean WEIRD-minus-Serbian difference
across the eight pairs was −0.013, or −1.3
percentage points (95% bootstrap CI: −8.1
to 5.8 points). An exact one-sided Wilcoxon
signed-rank test on the eight pair-level differ-
ences found no evidence for the hypothesised
WEIRD advantage (p= 0.680 ). Coverage
remained incomplete in both settings, and the
result does not establish equivalent cultural
coverage.
1 Introduction
An LLM answering a controversial political ques-
tion selects both facts and perspectives. An answer
about Kosovo’s independence, immigration, or his-
torical responsibility may be factually accurate yet
omit positions held by people involved in the de-
bate. As language models mediate access to po-
litical information, we therefore need to evaluate
which perspectives their answers recover.
Coverage may depend on how frequently posi-
tions appear in training data, how readily models
retrieve them, and whether post-training or safety
procedures discourage their expression. These pres-
sures may affect populations unevenly. The concen-
tration of language-model development in Western,
Educated, Industrialised, Rich, and Democratic
(WEIRD) and English-language settings motivates
concern about cultural representation (Sorensen
et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025;
Zewail et al., 2026). We therefore hypothesise that
models recover perspectives more completely on
WEIRD controversies than on Serbian controver-
sies.
Testing this expectation requires attention to
topic selection. Political controversies differ in
documentation, history, sensitivity, and structure.
A comparison of unrelated WEIRD and Serbian
topics could attribute differences in topic difficulty
to cultural setting. We reduce this ambiguity by
comparing structurally analogous controversies.
The benchmark contains 16 topics arranged
into eight WEIRD–Serbian pairs. Each topic
has five reference perspectives documented in sur-
veys, polling, referendum results, peer-reviewed
research, or clearly attested public debate. These
references provide a fixed evaluation target; they
do not exhaust the legitimate positions on an issue.
Thirteen LLMs each generate five perspectives per
topic in English. Five LLM judges independently
match each generated perspective to one of the ref-
erences or return no match. No judge evaluates a
generator from its own model family. Recall then
measures how much of the reference set the outputs
recover.
We address three research questions:
RQ1 (primary).Do LLMs recover fewer docu-
mented perspectives on Serbian sociopolitical top-
ics than on structurally matched WEIRD topics?
We hypothesise higher recall on WEIRD topics.

The paper makes three contributions:
• A matched benchmark:eight structurally
analogous WEIRD–Serbian topic pairs that
reduce confounding between cultural setting
and topic choice.
• An externally documented evaluation tar-

• A direct test of the WEIRD recall hypothe-

These findings motivate a more specific question
for subsequent evaluation: which perspectives do
models omit on which controversies?
2 Related work
Our study connects research on cultural representa-
tion, perspective coverage, and LLM-based evalua-
tion.
2.1 Pluralistic alignment and cultural
representation
Pluralistic alignment treats differences in human
values and viewpoints as something to preserve,
rather than collapse into a single preference target
(Sorensen et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2024; Ali et al.,
2025). This matters for political questions, where
a useful answer may need to represent conflicting
positions.
Sorensen et al. (2024) distinguish three forms of
pluralism. Overton pluralism concerns the range of
positions a model represents. Steerable pluralism
concerns its ability to adopt a requested position.
Distributional pluralism concerns whether output
frequencies reflect a target population. Our bench-
mark measures Overton-style coverage: whether
documented perspectives are recovered at all.
The concentration of training, preference, and
evaluation data in WEIRD and English-language
settings raises concerns about uneven cultural repre-
sentation (Sorensen et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025;
Zewail et al., 2026). Multilingual and culturally
adaptive prompting can improve diversity in some
settings (Wang et al., 2025). Culturally focused
benchmarks also document uneven resource cover-
age, including for Serbian and other South Slavic
languages (Perovic and Mihajlov, 2026). This work
motivates our directional hypothesis, but does not
determine whether a particular coverage difference
reflects cultural setting or the topics chosen for
comparison.
2.2 Measuring perspective coverage
Political controversies rarely have a complete, un-
contested set of correct labels. Perspectivist NLP
therefore treats disagreement as part of the phe-
nomenon being studied (Basile et al., 2021; Plank,
2022; Frenda et al., 2025). Related approaches
retain annotator-specific rationales or label distribu-
tions rather than combining all judgments into one
target (Sarumi et al., 2026; Shetty et al., 2026). A
fixed reference set can document positions known
to exist, but cannot guarantee an exhaustive account
of a debate.
Other work uses LLMs as proxies for human
opinion. Under some prompting strategies, mod-
els can reproduce population-level polling patterns
(Sun et al., 2024; Gong et al., 2026). Their ap-
parent political or cultural preferences can also
change with prompt design and methodology
(Khan et al., 2025). Hybrid approaches combine
model-generated annotations with human attention
to uncertain cases (Mehrotra et al., 2026). Our
evaluation instead uses externally documented ref-
erence perspectives, keeping the scoring target sep-

2.3 Evaluating generated perspectives
Evaluating coverage requires deciding whether dif-
ferently worded statements express the same stance.
LLM judges can assess this semantic relationship,
but their decisions may depend on presentation,
style, and model provenance (Poole-Dayan et al.,
2025; Chen et al., 2025).
We use five judges, present each generated per-
spective separately, randomise reference order, and
exclude judges from the generator’s model fam-
ily. We also report inter-judge agreement and com-
pare it across WEIRD and Serbian topics. These
checks assess consistency of the matching proce-
dure. They cannot establish correctness, because
several judges may share an error, and they do not
replace human validation.
3 Methodology
The benchmark fixes the topics and reference
perspectives before model outputs are evaluated.
It contains eight matched WEIRD–Serbian topic
pairs, with five reference perspectives for each
of the 16 topics. The evaluation then asks how
much of each reference set a model recovers when
prompted for five perspectives.
3.1 Selecting and matching topics
We began with Serbian sociopolitical controversies.
Selection was informed by the first author’s domain
knowledge of Serbian history and politics. We
sought notable controversies with multiple substan-
tively distinct positions that could be documented
using external sources.
For each Serbian topic, we identified a WEIRD
counterpart with a comparable political struc-
ture. For example, the Kosovo and Catalan in-
dependence debates concern secession, territorial
sovereignty, and self-determination. The Vu ˇci´c
and Orbán debates concern political leadership and
authoritarian or illiberal governance. Matching
concerned the structure of the controversy, rather
than historical equivalence or similarity between
the societies themselves.
The procedure was investigator-guided rather
than algorithmic. A counterpart also needed
enough external material to document distinct per-
spectives using the same construction procedure.
Table 1 lists the resulting pairs and their matching
rationales. Here, WEIRD and Serbian identify the
two benchmark groups; Serbia is the single non-
WEIRD setting studied. Matching reduces ambi-
guity due to topic selection without experimentally
isolating cultural setting.
3.2 Constructing the reference sets
A reference perspective is a substantively distinct
stance documented as being held within the target
population. We drew on surveys, polling, referen-
dum results, peer-reviewed research, and clearly
attested public debate. The reference statements
summarise positions supported by these sources,
rather than positions generated by the models eval-
uated here.
Each topic has five references so that all topics
share the same evaluation budget. This is a design
choice, not a claim that a controversy has exactly
five legitimate positions. The score measures re-
covery of these references; it does not measure
every position that might exist outside them. Each
stored perspective consists of a short summary and
a longer explanation. Judges receive both.
Table 2 shows an actual reference set. It illus-
trates why identifying a broad side of a controversy
is insufficient: support for fracking may be uncon-
ditional, conditional, or opposed to local develop-
ment; opposition may emphasise different grounds.
3.3 Evaluation procedure
The procedure separates generation, judging, and
scoring. Generators receive a topic and its tar-
get population, but not the reference perspectives.
Judges then compare each generated perspective
with the references. We calculate recall from their
assignments and use the resulting model–topic
scores for the model summaries and matched cul-
tural comparison.

sponse for each model–topic combination. The
prompt asks for five distinct perspectives span-

It is the primary metric because the research ques-

covering an additional reference.

4 Results
4.1 RQ1: Recall on matched WEIRD and
Serbian topics
Mean recall is 0.581 for WEIRD topics and 0.594
for Serbian topics. Across the eight matched topic
pairs, the mean WEIRD-minus-Serbian difference
is −0.013, or −1.3 percentage points (95% boot-
strap CI: −8.1 to 5.8 percentage points). The me-
dian pair-level difference is−0.014.
The exact one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test
provides no evidence for the hypothesised WEIRD
advantage (p= 0.680 ). The two-sided test gives
p= 0.742 , with a matched-pairs rank-biserial cor-
relation of −0.17. The point estimate favours Ser-
bian topics slightly, but the interval includes dif-
ferences in both directions. This result does not
establish equivalent coverage across the two set-

Four matched pairs favour WEIRD topics and

4.2 RQ2: Recall by model and access category
Table 4 reports perspective recall, with separate
columns for WEIRD and Serbian topics. Over-
all recall ranges from 0.458 for Llama 3.3 70B to
0.698 for Gemini 3.1 Pro. Even the highest-recall
model leaves approximately 30% of the reference
coverage unrecovered on average.
The mean recall of the selected models is 0.615
in the Frontier/API group, 0.578 in the larger open-
weight group, and0.548 in the smaller open-weight
group. Combining the eight open-weight models
gives mean recall 0.570, compared with 0.615 for
the five API systems, an observed difference of
4.5 percentage points. The exact pair-blocked test
gives p= 0.086 before correction and p= 0.258
after Holm correction across the four RQ2 con-
trasts. None of those four comparisons meets the
0.05 threshold after correction; the smallest ad-
justed value isp= 0.063 for the API versus smaller
open-weight comparison. These exploratory results
do not establish equivalence or isolate the effects
of size, openness, or capability.
Individual models also vary substantially within
categories. Gemma 4 31B has recall 0.638, close
to the 0.645 reported for GPT 5.5 and DeepSeek
V4 Pro, whereas Llama 3.3 70B has recall 0.458.
A category mean therefore does not describe every
model in that category. Overlapping individual
confidence intervals should not be interpreted as
evidence of equivalence.
4.3 RQ3: US and Chinese developer groups
The four selected models from Chinese develop-
ers have mean overall recall 0.609, compared with
0.579 for the eight models from US developers (Ta-
ble 5). The US-minus-China difference is −0.030
(95% pair-bootstrap CI [−0.048,−0.013] ). The
exploratory two-sided pair-blocked sign-flip test
gives p= 0.023 , or p= 0.047 after Holm correc-
tion across the two RQ3 outcomes.
The groups’ cultural gaps differ less conclu-
sively. The mean WEIRD-minus-Serbian gap is
−0.019 in the US group and +0.006 in the Chi-
nese group. Their difference is −0.025 (95%
pair-bootstrap CI [−0.082,0.025] ; raw and Holm-
adjusted p= 0.453 ). The observed overall-recall
difference therefore does not establish a corre-
sponding difference in the WEIRD-Serbian gap.
These results describe the selected systems. The
Chinese group contains one API and three larger
open-weight models, whereas the US group con-
tains four API, two larger open-weight, and two
smaller open-weight models. The US group also
includes multiple models from some developers.
Developer country is consequently entangled with
model selection and category composition. The
comparison does not establish a general advantage
of Chinese models or a causal effect of developer
location.
4.4 Descriptive checks on judge consistency
Agreement statistics have similar values for
WEIRD and Serbian topics (Table 6). Fleiss’ κ
is 0.761 for WEIRD topics and 0.774 for Serbian
topics. Gwet’s AC1 and Krippendorff’sα show the
same descriptive pattern. The judge-level no-match
rates are 32.9% and 31.1%, respectively.
Removing one judge at a time leaves the highest-
recall model unchanged and produces small
changes in the pooled WEIRD-minus-Serbian
gap, which ranges from approximately −0.020
to −0.008 across removals. These checks pro-
vide no obvious indication of substantially lower
agreement for Serbian topics or dependence of the
pooled result on one judge. They are descriptive
checks, not formal demonstrations that the panels
have equal error rates. Several judges may agree
on an incorrect match, and shared cultural biases
remain possible.
5 Discussion
The primary comparison found no detectable
WEIRD recall advantage in this benchmark. Large
differences between individual topic pairs occurred
in both directions, while coverage remained incom-
plete in both cultural settings. The results therefore
motivate attention to topic selection and to the par-
ticular perspectives that models omit.

Neither the pooled analysis nor the per-
model comparisons supports systematically higher
WEIRD recall. Agreement among judges was also
similar across the two settings, with no obvious
indication of substantially noisier matching on Ser-
bian material. These findings concern the selected
controversies under direct elicitation in English.
They do not establish equal representation across
cultures, languages, or tasks.
Several explanations remain possible. Con-
temporary training data may contain enough re-
gional and multilingual material to recover well-
documented Serbian positions when models are
explicitly asked for diverse perspectives. Retrieval
limitations or post-training behaviour may con-
strain coverage across controversies more generally.
The five-perspective output limit also creates com-
petition among legitimate positions for inclusion.
The experiment does not distinguish these explana-
tions.
Topic selection matters when interpreting the
result. Variation across controversies was much
larger than the pooled cultural difference, with
large pair differences favouring both settings. An
unmatched comparison could misattribute such
variation to cultural setting. Structural matching
reduces this ambiguity, although it cannot remove
all differences between controversies.
5.2 Incomplete coverage remains
The absence of a detectable WEIRD advantage
does not imply comprehensive coverage. Even the
strongest models omit a substantial share of the
reference perspectives. In this benchmark, omis-
sions occur across both cultural settings rather than
consistently concentrating in one.
Model performance also overlaps across access
and size categories, and many differences among
middle-ranked systems remain statistically unre-
solved. These categories are therefore poor substi-
tutes for measuring perspective coverage directly.
Appendix D.9 reports descriptive summaries of
perspective extremity; these do not establish why
coverage varies across topics.
Future work could examine what makes particu-
lar controversies or perspectives difficult to recover.
Candidate factors include the amount and visibility
of documentation, the prominence of positions in
training data, and their responsiveness to elicitation
after post-training. Measuring these properties di-
rectly would allow them to be tested as predictors,
rather than inferred from cultural or model-level
averages.
5.3 Implications for evaluation and culturally
adaptive systems
The matched design can support comparisons in-
volving other populations. Its purpose is to make
differences in perspective coverage easier to inter-
pret by accounting for topic selection. Coverage
should also be evaluated directly: general capabil-
ity, multilingual performance, and overall answer
quality do not establish that a model will recover
the positions relevant to a contested issue.
The appropriate intervention depends on the ob-
served deficit. A systematic cultural coverage gap
may motivate targeted cultural or linguistic adap-
tation. If coverage is incomplete across both sides
of a matched comparison, improving the retrieval
or elicitation of omitted perspectives may be more
relevant. The present result does not make cultural
adaptation unnecessary; it helps specify what an
intervention would need to address.
This distinction also matters for AI-mediated de-
liberation. Systems that expose disagreement, sup-
port consensus, or introduce minority positions de-
pend on the perspectives available to them (Tessler
et al., 2024; Chiang et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024;
Lee et al., 2025). A perspective that never enters
the candidate set cannot inform those later pro-

6 Limitations
The findings apply to a finite reference benchmark,
a selected set of models and topics, and one elicita-
tion and judging procedure.
6.1 The reference sets are not exhaustive
Each topic has five externally documented refer-
ence perspectives. This common target supports
comparison across topics, but it cannot represent
every legitimate position within a population. Re-
call therefore measures coverage of the reference
set. Similarly, an output that matches no reference
receives no precision credit even if it expresses a
legitimate position outside the benchmark. Abso-
lute recall and precision values should be read in
this context; their main value here is comparison
under a common construction procedure.
6.2 Judge agreement does not establish judge
validity
The scores depend on LLM judgments of whether
generated and reference perspectives express the
same stance. High and similar agreement across
WEIRD and Serbian topics supports the consis-
tency of this procedure. It does not establish cor-
rectness: judges may share errors, and we do not
have an independent human-annotated answer key
for every matching decision.
Excluding judges from the generator’s family
and repeating the analysis after removing individ-
ual judges reduce dependence on particular mod-
els. They do not validate the judgments. Human
validation of a stratified sample of matched and un-
matched outputs would strengthen the benchmark.
6.3 Eight matched pairs limit statistical
resolution
The primary comparison contains eight matched
topic pairs. Although the estimated WEIRD-minus-
Serbian difference is close to zero, its 95% confi-
dence interval extends from approximately −0.081
to 0.058. The data remain compatible with modest
differences in either direction. We therefore re-
port no detectable WEIRD recall advantage in this
benchmark, rather than equality between settings.
The small topic sample also limits exploratory anal-
yses of properties such as perspective extremity,
whose results remain descriptive.
6.4 Serbia is one non-WEIRD setting
The Serbian controversies studied here are Euro-
pean, relatively well documented, and often dis-
cussed in multilingual or internationally indexed
sources. They represent one non-WEIRD setting.
The findings cannot be generalised to all non-
WEIRD populations. Larger coverage gaps may
occur in less documented societies, lower-resource
languages, or political contexts with less interna-
tional attention. Matched comparisons involving
additional populations are needed to test this possi-
bility.
6.5 The paper tests direct elicitation at one
point in time
We explicitly asked models for diverse perspec-
tives. The benchmark therefore measures recovery
under direct elicitation, rather than the perspectives
models would spontaneously include in ordinary
conversation. All prompts were in English, holding
elicitation and judging language constant across
settings. The study does not test Serbian-language
or multilingual prompting, which may produce dif-
ferent coverage patterns.
The results also reflect one snapshot of changing
model systems. Rankings may change as those sys-
tems are updated. The matched design is intended
to remain useful beyond the evaluated model ver-
sions.
6.6 Structural matching reduces but does not
eliminate topic confounding
The pairs share broad political structures, but may
differ in documentation, historical depth, polarisa-
tion, and training-data prevalence. Matching im-
proves the interpretability of the comparison with-
out experimentally isolating cultural setting. It was
investigator-guided rather than based on a prespec-
ified quantitative rule, and alternative pairings may
be defensible.
Complete topic definitions, matching rationales,
reference perspectives, and supporting sources are
needed to make these choices inspectable and sup-
port alternative pairings in future benchmark exten-
sions.

and 13 generators, we did not detect the predicted
WEIRD recall advantage. Coverage remained in-

F Judge reliability and robustness
F.1 Agreement coefficients
We report three agreement coefficients because
their assumptions differ. Fleiss’κ is a conventional
multi-rater agreement statistic but can be sensitive
to skewed marginal distributions. Gwet’s AC1 is
less sensitive to this problem. Krippendorff’s α
provides an additional nominal-scale measure and
accommodates missing ratings. Missing ratings are
excluded item-wise; Fleiss’κ and Gwet’s AC1 use
the variable-rater formulations implemented in the
analysis pipeline.
Table 6 reports all three coefficients overall and
by cultural group.
The three coefficients give similar results, and
agreement is similar across the two cultural arms.
These statistics measure consistency, not correct-
ness.
F.2 Pairwise judge agreement
Pairwise Cohen’sκ is calculated for each pair of
base-panel judges using only generated perspec-
tives rated by both judges. The number of co-rated
perspectives ranges from approximately720 to 880
across judge pairs. Pairwise κ values range from ap-
proximately 0.67 to 0.85. The three lowest values
are 0.671 for Gemini-Claude, 0.684 for Gemini-
GPT, and 0.695 for Gemini-DeepSeek. These re-
sults show that judge decisions are related but not
identical; they do not identify which judge is more
accurate.
F.3 Leave-one-judge-out robustness
We repeat the aggregation after removing each of
the seven models that appears anywhere in the
judge roster. The remaining valid judgments are
re-averaged before recall, precision, and F1 are
recalculated.
Spearman correlations between the full-panel
and leave-one-judge-out recall rankings range from
0.971 to 0.999. The corresponding F1-ranking
correlations range from 0.978 to 1.000. Gemini
3.1 Pro remains first by F1 under every exclu-
sion. The pooled WEIRD-minus-Serbian recall
difference ranges from approximately −0.008 to
−0.020, compared with −0.013 in the full anal-
ysis; the corresponding two-sided p-values range
from approximately 0.38 to 0.84. The largest indi-
vidual change in recall is 3.6 percentage points for
Llama 3.3 70B when the Gemini judge is removed.
F.4 No-match decisions
Across all valid judge decisions, 1,658 of 5,184
are no-match votes, giving a judge-level no-match
vote rate of 32.0%. The corresponding rates are
32.9% for WEIRD topics and 31.1% for Serbian
topics.
